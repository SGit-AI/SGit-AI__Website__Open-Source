Where we lose

This site is published by the sgit project — a company that runs entirely on the model it is arguing for, sells services built on it, and would benefit commercially if you agreed. That is a participant publishing an argument about its own market, and it should change how you read this. So here is what to discount, and where our own approach is worse than the alternatives.

The interest, stated plainly

  • The estate is entirely open source — Apache-2.0 on the code, CC BY 4.0 on the documents, CC0 on the published articles. An argument that open source is a winning strategy is an argument that our strategy is winning.
  • The commercial model is selling maintenance, quality and certification rather than the code. The villagers argument is a description of a market we intend to be in.
  • The survivability argument is a reason to prefer structurally-open vendors. We are one — while failing three of its four legs ourselves.

How much to discount, section by section

The discipline applies unevenly, and pretending otherwise would itself be a rhetorical move. So:

SectionHow much our interest should worry you
The historyVery little. These claims are externally verifiable and cited, and several of them cut against the romantic story that would flatter the argument. If we had wanted a convenient history we would not have written the one where the movement's founding case study is a six-year near-failure. Check it; it does not depend on trusting us.
The numbersVery little, and the refusals list is the reason: the numbers we decline to publish would have helped us. "Linux runs 90% of the cloud" is a great line for a site like this one, and it has no source.
The stress testSome — and it is structurally mitigated. The four legs are answerable from public artefacts, so the tool works without us and cannot be tuned in our favour. We deliberately do not publish verdicts on named competitors, because that would convert a diagnostic into a weapon held by an interested party.
The argumentA lot. This is where our interest is most direct, and it is why every argument on this site carries its counter-case — not as balance for its own sake, but because an argument published by an interested party with no visible objections is advertising.
FundingA lot, and in a specific direction: we would benefit from a world in which companies pay third parties to maintain their open-source dependencies. The position taken is one we would profit from.
The self-auditRead it as the test of everything else. If we would not run our own tool on ourselves, nothing above is worth much.

Where our own approach loses

Not caveats — places where a reader would be right to choose differently.

We fail our own stress test, and cannot fully fix it

Three of four legs fail. Single-company copyright, single-company trademark, no named fork capacity. And the uncomfortable structural point: a project with no external contributors cannot have distributed copyright, so the position that makes our approach coherent is the same one that produces the failure. They are one gap wearing two hats, and no amount of good intent changes it.

We argue for a discipline we have not practised

The survivability argument turns on DCO versus CLA in practice — and we have never run a project with external contributors under either. We are making a confident structural claim from outside the experience it describes. Someone who has maintained a large distributed-copyright project for a decade knows things about this that we do not.

We tell companies to declare a supply chain we have not declared

One manual licence review, from February 2026. No SBOM, no automated scanning, no policy, no upstream-contribution record. This is the plainest inconsistency on the site. It is a day's work, and it is not done.

A commons built by people who disagreed with us

We depend on software written largely by people who thought open source was about the community and about giving back — the framing this site opens by rejecting. The strategic argument may well be the better description of why the model works. It is not why most of the code we run got written, and there is something owed there that a strategy argument has no vocabulary for.

The site is new, and the argument is not yet load-bearing anywhere

None of the three funding proposals has been piloted. The stress test has not been run at scale by anyone but us. The villagers market is a forecast. This is a site of arguments, published early, with its open questions visible — which is the honest state of it.

Why publish this at all

Because the alternative is worse, and because the corpus already names the cost in its own words: "Publishing security reviews — it is unusually transparent and it publishes your own weaknesses to an audience that includes people looking for them."

That is a real cost, paid in public. It is also the only thing that makes the transparency claim worth anything: a disclosure that discloses nothing inconvenient is a marketing asset, and a self-audit that could not fail is not evidence. The parts of this site most likely to be quoted against us — the self-audit, the missing SBOM, the community gap — are the parts that make the rest checkable.

If you find something wrong here, we would rather know. The repository is public, the source briefs are published verbatim, and the comms board is where corrections and requests are tracked in the open.
